A first-timer's guide to Anna Maria Island.

Where to park, when to go, the streets locals would actually point you to, and the off-island detour that makes the trip.

By Devon W. · Anna Maria local · Updated April 2026
ANNA MARIA · LEAD IMAGE

Anna Maria Island is seven miles long and three miles wide. It has three small cities — Anna Maria, Holmes Beach, Bradenton Beach — none of which feel like a city. Building heights are capped, chains are kept out, and the whole island runs on a free trolley that swings end-to-end every twenty minutes.

I've lived here for fourteen years. This is the guide I wish someone had handed me before my first visit.

Don't drive onto the island. (Or do, but only once.)

The cardinal sin of an Anna Maria visit is bringing your rental car onto the island and trying to find parking at three different beaches. Don't. Park at Coquina Beach in the morning — there's plenty of room — and use the trolley for the rest of your day.

If your dates are before Memorial Day or after Labor Day, you can ignore this advice. If your dates are summer or holiday weekend, please don't.

The other way in

The Gulf Islands Ferry runs from downtown Bradenton's Riverwalk to Bridge Street. Forty-five minutes on the water, no bridge traffic, and the ferry itself is part of the trip. The Perks Pass bundles the ride with restaurant discounts and a trolley day-pass.

Where to start your first morning

Two options, depending on energy level:

  • The Donut Experiment in Holmes Beach. Made-to-order donuts. Worth the line. Coffee is fine, but you came for the donuts.
  • Rod & Reel Pier on the north end. Casual breakfast on the working pier, with the boats coming in. Order the eggs and watch the water.

Beaches, in order of personality

People always ask which beach. The answer is "all of them, in different moods."

  • Coquina Beach — long stretch, big lots, pavilion grills. The "have everything" beach. Best for an all-day setup with kids.
  • Manatee Public Beach — concession, lifeguard, restrooms. Family-ready. Walk-up cafe is solid.
  • Cortez Beach — no-frills sand and dunes. Walk-on, low-traffic. Best sunsets on the island.
  • Bean Point — where the Gulf meets the Bay. Quiet, scenic, locals' favorite. Limited parking, so if you can trolley up to Pine Avenue and walk, do.

Pine Avenue, slowly

Pine Avenue is the closest thing the island has to a Main Street, and locals call it "the greenest little Main Street in America." Spend at least one slow afternoon there — small shops, locally-owned everything, and three or four places to sit with a coffee or a beer and watch the day move.

Where to eat without a reservation drama

You'll get conflicting advice from every local you meet. Here's the version that won't steer you wrong:

  • Sunset dinner with reservations: The Sandbar or Beach House Restaurant — both put your table on the sand.
  • Sunset dinner without reservations: Rod & Reel Pier · arrive at 5:30, eat by 6, watch the sun do its thing by 7.
  • Floribbean date night: The Reef House on Bridge Street. Hogfish, key lime, deck over the pass. More Floribbean tables here.
  • Casual cocktails: Doctor's Office — tiny converted clinic, cocktail-and-small-plates. Locals' choice.

The off-island detour that makes the trip

Drive ten minutes to Cortez Village — Florida's last working commercial fishing village. Eat at Star Fish Company (order at the window, picnic on the dock). Walk through the Florida Maritime Museum in the 1912 schoolhouse. Watch the boats unload. Buy a piece of fish to cook in your rental.

Cortez is what Anna Maria looked like before it was Anna Maria.

What to skip

I'm going to disappoint you here, because every "what to do" list says to go to all of these. You don't need to:

  • The "haunted" pirate attractions. (We don't have any pirates.)
  • Any chain restaurant. (You didn't fly here for that.)
  • Trying to drive to Egmont Key. (You can't drive there. You take a boat.)

One last thing

Build into your trip a single hour, on a single beach, doing nothing. No phone. No itinerary. The whole reason this island stays the way it is — low-rise, low-key, all-Florida — is that the people who love it have made a deliberate choice to keep it that way.

You will, too. After an hour.
