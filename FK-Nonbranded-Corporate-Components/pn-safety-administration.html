Safe Practices for Parenteral Nutrition

PN is classified as a high-alert medication due to its complex formulation, narrow therapeutic index, and potential for serious adverse events when administered incorrectly. Patient safety requires standardized protocols covering order verification, compounding, labeling, administration, and ongoing monitoring.

Spotlight Callout

Safety designation

PN is an ISMP-classified high-alert medication.

High-alert medications carry a heightened risk of causing significant patient harm when used in error. Organizations should implement robust safeguards — standardized order sets, independent double-checks, and smart pump technology — to minimize PN-related adverse events.

Administration Safety Checklist

Before initiating any PN infusion, clinicians should systematically verify each element of the following safety checklist. This structured approach reduces the risk of medication errors and ensures optimal patient outcomes.

Feature Card

1. Verify the PN order against patient-specific requirements

Confirm that the prescribed macronutrient and micronutrient composition aligns with the patient's metabolic needs, weight, renal function, and fluid status. Perform an independent double-check of all calculations.

Feature Card

2. Confirm line placement and patency

Verify that the vascular access device is properly positioned and patent. For central PN, confirm tip placement via chest radiograph before initiating the infusion. Assess for signs of occlusion or malposition.

Feature Card

3. Check solution integrity and expiration

Inspect the PN solution for particulate matter, precipitates, color changes, or signs of lipid emulsion instability (creaming, oiling out). Verify the expiration date and confirm the solution has been stored at the appropriate temperature.

Feature Card

4. Use proper infusion pump settings and rates

Program the infusion pump with the prescribed rate and volume. Utilize smart pump dose-error reduction software (DERS) with PN-specific drug libraries. Verify rate limits and set appropriate alarms.

Feature Card

5. Monitor for adverse reactions during first 30 minutes

Closely observe the patient during the initial infusion period for signs of allergic reaction, fever, rigors, tachycardia, or respiratory distress. Assess vital signs at baseline and at 15- and 30-minute intervals.

Vascular Access & Line Care

The choice of vascular access device depends on the expected duration of PN therapy, osmolarity of the solution, and the patient's vascular anatomy. Proper line care is essential for preventing catheter-related complications.

Tool Card

Central Venous Catheters

PICC lines, tunneled catheters (e.g., Hickman, Broviac), and implanted ports provide reliable central access for high-osmolarity PN solutions intended for extended duration therapy.

Tool Card

Peripheral Access

Peripheral PN is limited to solutions with osmolarity <900 mOsm/L. Higher concentrations increase the risk of phlebitis and vessel damage. Peripheral access is appropriate only for short-term supplemental nutrition.

Tool Card

Line Maintenance

Adherence to standardized dressing change protocols, proper flushing with heparin or saline, and aseptic technique during all line access are critical for infection prevention and catheter longevity.

Infection Prevention

Catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs) remain one of the most serious complications of parenteral nutrition therapy. Implementing an evidence-based prevention bundle can significantly reduce infection rates.

Callout

CRBSI Prevention Bundle

The following evidence-based interventions should be implemented as a bundle to minimize catheter-related bloodstream infection risk: hand hygiene with alcohol-based antiseptic before and after all line access; maximal sterile barrier precautions during catheter insertion; chlorhexidine-based skin antisepsis at the insertion site; optimal catheter site selection — avoid femoral vein for central access; daily review of line necessity with prompt removal of unnecessary catheters; use of closed catheter access systems and needleless connectors; and dedicated PN lumen — do not use the PN line for blood draws or medication administration.

Transitioning Off PN

Weaning from parenteral nutrition should be a gradual, clinically guided process. PN should not be abruptly discontinued, as this may cause rebound hypoglycemia due to the sudden withdrawal of the high dextrose load. The infusion rate should be tapered over 1–2 hours before discontinuation.

Transition to enteral or oral nutrition should begin as soon as gastrointestinal function permits. PN can typically be reduced and eventually discontinued once the patient is tolerating ≥60% of their estimated nutritional requirements via the enteral route. Close monitoring of nutritional intake, weight trends, and laboratory values should continue throughout the weaning process.

Criteria for PN discontinuation include adequate oral or enteral intake, resolution of the underlying condition necessitating PN, hemodynamic stability, and normalized laboratory parameters including glucose, electrolytes, and hepatic function markers.

Critical edge case

Central vs. peripheral access changes the formulation rules.

PN admixtures with osmolarity above ~900 mOsm/L require central venous access. Below that threshold, peripheral PN is an option for short-term support — usually under two weeks.
